Documentation

The playground is a small fake application used across the accessibility series. It exists in two versions that look the same: the accessible version is the reference, and the seeded version has deliberate bugs to find. Nothing you enter in either is saved, and reloading restores the bugs.

Each session below lists what the facilitator covers and the mission participants get. Sessions are filled in as the series goes along.

Sessions

Session 1: Kick-off – accessibility, WCAG 2.2, Level AA, resources and tools

Held on 15 October 2026.

Goal: know who accessibility serves, how WCAG 2.2 is built and how to read a success criterion, and try a real task with the keyboard only.

Agenda

  1. What accessibility is. Making the product usable by people who perceive, operate or understand it differently: permanently, temporarily or in a situation. Who benefits from an accessible website.
  2. Assistive technologies. Screen readers, magnifiers and browser zoom, keyboard only, switch devices, voice control and braille displays, and what each one needs from the code. Demo: NVDA on a documentation site.
  3. WCAG 2.2. Four principles, 13 guidelines and 86 success criteria. Levels A, AA and AAA, and why the target is AA. How to read a success criterion and find its Understanding page.
  4. The champion role. What a champion does and does not do, and who owns a finding.
  5. Four criteria on a real screen. Bypass Blocks (2.4.1), Page Titled (2.4.2), Focus Order (2.4.3) and Focus Visible (2.4.7).
  6. Mission. Participants complete a task in the product with the keyboard only.
  7. Reflection. A poll on the biggest barrier, then three questions: where the focus got lost, whether it moved in a sensible order and came back, and how many Tab stops the task took.

Mission: complete a task with the keyboard only

This session did not use the playground. Use the product you work on, or any site with a flow that opens a dialog and a form. Unplug the mouse or switch off the trackpad.

  1. Load the page and press Tab once. What received focus first?
  2. Reach the button that starts the task and press Enter. Where did the focus land?
  3. In the dialog, Tab through it, then press Escape. Where does the focus return?
  4. Open the dialog again and complete it. Where did the focus go after the page changed?
  5. Fill in the required fields and Tab through the rest of the form to the Save button. Could you see the focus indicator at every Tab stop?
  6. Save, then go back with Shift+Tab to the Back button and press Enter. Where did the focus land?

Before Session 2, set up the bookmarklets, axe DevTools and the Colour Contrast Analyser from the links below, and check that they work.

Session 2: ARIA, the accessibility tree and name, role, value

Held on 22 October 2026.

Goal: know what a screen reader needs from every control, a name, a role and a state, where each one comes from, and three ways to check it.

Agenda

  1. Name, role, value. What each one is, and why every control has to expose all three (4.1.2).
  2. Semantic elements, and div and span. What a native element gives you for free, and what a div with a click handler does not.
  3. ARIA. It changes what is announced, never what the element does. The first rule: a native element first, ARIA only for the gaps. How aria-labelledby builds a name.
  4. The accessibility tree. The second tree the browser builds from the DOM, the CSS and ARIA. How to read name, role and state in the Accessibility pane of Chrome DevTools, and the order in which the name is computed.
  5. Demo: axe DevTools. A scan of the seeded Agents page, and why a suggested fix has to be read before it is applied.
  6. Mission. Participants test the Agents page in four passes: mouse, axe, keyboard, tree.
  7. Review together. What each pass found, and what automation misses.

Mission: the Agents page in four passes

Use the Agents page of the seeded version in Card view, reloaded before you start. List view and the New agent form are out of scope. Count the issues you find in each pass. For each one, write down where it is, what is wrong, how you found it, and the WCAG success criterion if you know it.

  1. Mouse. Explore the page the way you normally would: click the view toggle, hover the buttons, open an agent, look at the browser tab.
  2. axe. Run axe DevTools on the Agents page and read each result: the rule, the element, the suggested fix.
  3. Keyboard. Hands off the mouse. Tab through everything you see, open and operate the More actions menu, type in the search box and Tab on, open an agent and come back.
  4. Tree. In the Accessibility pane, inspect four elements: the Card and List view selector, the search input, the disabled New agent button, and one agent card with its button and menu. Read off the name, the role and the state of each.

You may get stuck without a mouse. That is a finding, not a fault of the playground: note where it happened, use the mouse to continue, and carry on.

Afterwards, pick one control, for example the More actions menu on a card. Inspect it in the Accessibility pane on the seeded version, then on the accessible version. Compare the name, the role and the state, and find the markup that changed.

Check your findings: when you are done, compare your notes with the seeded bugs on the Agents page and the ones on every page.

Session 3: Landmarks, headings and language

Held on 29 October 2026.

Goal: make the structure a screen reader user skims by, the landmarks, the headings and the declared language, match the structure you see.

Agenda

  1. Why page structure matters. A sighted user skims a page by its layout and its headings. A screen reader user skims it by the landmarks, the headings and the declared language in the code.
  2. Landmarks. The landmark roles and their native elements, which ones appear once per page, which ones need a name, and when a section or a form becomes a landmark.
  3. Headings. One h1 that says what the page is, no level skipped, nothing styled as a heading without being one, and no heading used for its looks.
  4. Language. The lang attribute on the page and on every part in another language, with a language code, and what a language switcher has to change.
  5. Demo: the home page. The accessible home page with NVDA: the D and H keys and the elements list. Then the same page through the bookmarklets and ANDI.
  6. Mission. Participants examine the Settings page in four steps: look, tree, tools, findings.
  7. Walkthrough together. The Settings page step by step, and what each tool showed.

Mission: the Settings page in four steps

Use the Settings page of the seeded version. It only displays information, so you are looking at how the page is built, not at what it says.

  1. Look. Look at the page for ten seconds. Write down the heading outline you expect, the page heading and its sections in order, and the landmarks you expect: which content would sit in a banner, a navigation, main, an aside, a footer.
  2. Tree. Open DevTools, select an element, open the Accessibility pane and switch on the full-page accessibility tree. Walk it from the top and expand what is collapsed. Check every landmark and its name, and every heading and its level, against step 1.
  3. Tools. Run axe DevTools with Best practices switched on and read each result: the rule, the element, the fix. Then run the Structures module of ANDI. Then, if you have time, the Headings, Landmarks and Lang bookmarklets.
  4. Findings. For each issue, write down where it is, what is wrong, how you found it, and the WCAG success criterion you think it fails.

Afterwards, run the same tools on the Settings page of the accessible version and compare the landmarks and the heading outline.

Check your findings: when you are done, compare your notes with the seeded bugs on the Settings page and the ones on every page.

Session 4: Accessibility design review

Held on 5 November 2026.

Goal: review a design before it is built. Find what has to change, what has to be clarified, and what has to be implemented even though the mockup does not show it.

Agenda

  1. Why universal design. Success criteria are the minimum. The seven principles of universal design ask whether everyone can reach the same goal with the product.
  2. The review process. Five steps, every time you receive a design: understand the journey, identify problems, identify missing information, plan invisible accessibility, decide what happens next.
  3. Identify problems. What could make this design inaccessible or unnecessarily difficult to use? Text contrast, non-text contrast, content that moves on its own, unnecessary complexity.
  4. Identify missing information. What do you need to clarify before implementing? A desktop frame says nothing about other widths and zoom; a happy path says nothing about invalid values, failed saves, loading and empty states; a chart says nothing about how someone gets the values without seeing it.
  5. Plan invisible accessibility. What has to be built although the mockup does not show it: a way to bypass blocks, semantic structure, accessible names, status announcements, focus management, errors that are announced and not only shown. Check what the app shell and the component library already provide first.
  6. Decide what happens next. Every finding ends in one of three actions: fix, clarify or implement.
  7. Demo: how to check. Text contrast with a contrast checker, non-text contrast with the DevTools colour picker, and reflow at 320 CSS pixels with browser zoom rather than the device toolbar.
  8. Mission. Participants review the Monitoring page of the seeded version on their own, as the design they have just received.
  9. Review together. A poll on where the concerns were, then the page reviewed together.

Mission: is this design ready for development?

Use the Monitoring page of the seeded version. Treat it as a design handed over for implementation, not as a finished screen. The About this design button at the top right says what the designer provided and what was left unspecified. Private notes are enough; there is nothing to submit.

  1. Journey. Review the system metrics, filter the services, edit an alert threshold, save the change.
  2. Problems. Write down what could make this design inaccessible or unnecessarily difficult to use.
  3. Missing information. Write down what you would need to clarify with the designer or the product manager before implementing it.
  4. Invisible accessibility. Write down what must be implemented that the design does not show.
  5. Next step. For each note, decide: fix, clarify or implement.

Afterwards, open the Monitoring page of the accessible version, the revised proposal after design review. Compare it with your notes: what changed, and which of your points it answers.

Some findings are WCAG failures. Others are usability concerns that still create barriers. Learn to tell them apart.

Optional, if you want more to review: the button at the bottom of the page, Load more panels, adds a second batch on both versions: incidents, a comparison chart, recent deploys, a small form and panel settings. Same questions, same comparison.

Check your findings: when you are done, compare your notes with the seeded bugs on the Monitoring page and the ones on every page.

Session 5: Keyboard interaction patterns

Held on 12 November 2026.

Goal: know the keys each control answers to, from the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide, and tell a WCAG failure from a missing convention key.

Agenda

  1. The keys users expect. Tab and Shift+Tab between components, the arrow keys inside one, Enter and Space to activate, Escape to close and return the focus, Home, End, Page Up and Page Down to jump.
  2. A WCAG failure or a best practice. WCAG asks that everything works with the keyboard (2.1.1, 2.1.2, 2.4.3, 2.4.7, 3.2.1, 3.2.2). The APG says which keys.
  3. Native first. Which controls get their keys from the browser, and which have no native element and need every key in script: tabs, toolbar, tree, grid, menubar, menu button, combobox.
  4. The APG. What the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide is: patterns, working examples, and the practices behind them.
  5. Custom keyboard shortcuts. Why single-key shortcuts clash with screen reader keys, and what 2.1.4 asks for.
  6. The cheat sheet in five groups. One Tab stop each, arrow keys that change a value, composites, popups, and controls with no keys of their own.
  7. Mission. Participants explore the Components page of the accessible version with the keyboard only: they try each control first, then read its keys.
  8. Debrief. Which keys surprised you, and when to use a grid rather than a table.

Mission: try every control, then read its keys

Use the Components page of the accessible version. It shows every control pattern from the keyboard cheat sheet, grouped the same way and built the right way. The info button at the bottom right of each card lists the keys the control answers to. It comes after the control in the Tab order, so you reach the control first. Put the mouse away.

  1. Try. Tab to the control and operate it with the keys you expect: Enter, Space, Escape, the arrow keys, Home, End, Page Up and Page Down. Check a checkbox, expand a section, open a popup and close it again.
  2. Read. Tab on to the info button and compare its list with what you tried.
  3. Repeat. Every card, group by group, in the order of the page.

Note every key or control that surprised you.

A key that does nothing is a WCAG finding when it leaves something unreachable or inoperable (2.1.1), traps the focus (2.1.2), or moves it somewhere surprising (2.4.3, 3.2.1, 3.2.2). A missing convention key fails the APG pattern. For more practice, the same page in the seeded version has the controls built the way they often are: try them with the same keys and decide for each gap whether it fails WCAG or only the pattern.

Check your findings: when you are done, compare your notes with the seeded bugs on the Components page and the ones on every page.
